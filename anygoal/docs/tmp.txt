

## 1. The Problem: Learning the World or Learning the Goal?

A world model should predict the consequences of an action, regardless of the goal that motivated it.

However, expert demonstrations contain strong behavioral biases. Actions are not sampled independently of states. Instead, they are selected to accomplish a particular task.

Consider a robot trained to push a block toward a target. When the robot is far from the block, expert demonstrations almost always show it moving toward the block.

A world model trained on these trajectories may learn that the robot *typically approaches the block*, rather than learning what happens under an arbitrary supplied action.

**What if we change the goal from pushing the block to avoiding it?**

The environment, objects, and available actions remain the same. Yet the world model may fail because the new goal requires state–action combinations rarely encountered in expert rollouts.

In our Push-T example, world models that achieve substantial planning success on the original task suffer sharp degradation when evaluated on Avoid-T.

Targeted action interventions also reveal reduced model sensitivity in behaviorally biased regions, suggesting that the models can rely on expert-rollout regularities rather than reusable action-conditioned dynamics.

## 2. What Should World Models Generalize To?

### Familiar Components, Unseen Combinations

A world model cannot realistically observe every possible combination of states and actions during training.

But should it need to?

We argue that a fundamental generalization requirement is to **recombine familiar physical factors and actions in configurations not observed during training**.

Suppose the training data contain several physical factors, such as agent position, object position, and action. Each factor may be individually familiar, while their particular combination is novel.

We define the *combinatorial space* as the Cartesian product of the marginal training supports.

\[
\mathcal C^\times
=
\left(
\prod_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal Z_k^{\mathrm{tr}}
\right)
\times
\mathcal A^{\mathrm{tr}}.
\]

The *combinatorial gap* consists of combinations in this space that are absent or weakly covered by the joint training distribution.

AnyGoal targets this gap.

Rather than testing extrapolation to entirely new physical factors or actions, we evaluate whether learned dynamics remain valid under **new combinations of already observed factors**.

A goal shift changes which state–action combinations are visited, while preserving the underlying environment dynamics.

## 3. Introducing AnyGoal

### A Controlled Benchmark for Unseen Goals

AnyGoal evaluates world models on new goals within familiar environments.

It builds on four widely used world-model tasks.

| Base environment | Training goal | Example unseen goals |
|---|---|---|
| **TwoRoom** | Navigate through a narrow passage | TR-Route |
| **Push-T** | Push a T-shaped block to a target pose | Avoid-T, Stack-T, Push-T Antigoal |
| **Manipulator Insert-Peg** | Insert a peg into a target slot | Avoid-Peg, Extract-Peg |
| **OGBench Cube** | Pick and place a cube | Cube-Hop, Cube-Diagonal |

Together, these environments provide **eight goal-shifted variants**, spanning free-space navigation and contact-rich manipulation.

Each variant changes the behavioral objective without changing the environment's underlying dynamics.

Crucially, **world models are trained only on the original base tasks**. The AnyGoal variants are used exclusively for evaluating generalization.

AnyGoal provides 400 evaluation episodes per variant, together with tools for generating additional datasets.

**[Figure 3 — AnyGoal task overview]**

### Controlled Shifts, Not Arbitrary OOD Tests

How do we know that an unseen goal is not simply introducing unfamiliar states or actions?

AnyGoal explicitly checks whether each task-relevant factor remains marginally within the training distribution.

We use three complementary support checks.

- **Range**, verifying coverage within observed coordinate ranges.
- **Mahalanobis distance**, measuring consistency with the reference distribution's covariance structure.
- **Kernel density estimation**, checking empirical distributional coverage.

All eight variants pass the prescribed factor-wise support checks.

However, when we examine the **joint state–action distribution**, many variants have substantially less overlap with the corresponding training distribution.

This is the distinction AnyGoal is designed to capture.

**Individual factors remain familiar, but their combinations can be novel.**

## 4. Evaluating World Models Under Goal Shifts

We evaluate world models across different learning objectives, prediction spaces, and representation architectures.

Our model suite includes JEPA-style world models, generative world models, and predictors operating directly on oracle physical states.

We examine two complementary measures.

**Rollout prediction error** measures how accurately a world model predicts future transitions under supplied actions.

**MPC planning success** measures whether the learned model supports downstream planning toward the evaluation goal.

Models are evaluated on both their original training tasks and AnyGoal variants, allowing us to measure performance changes relative to each model's base-task performance.

This distinction matters because accurate rollout prediction and successful planning do not always agree.

## 5. What Do the Results Tell Us?

### Finding 1. Dynamics Fail Even with Oracle States

Is goal-shift failure primarily a problem of visual representation?

To investigate this, we evaluate **oracle-state predictors** that receive task-relevant physical state variables directly, removing the need to infer them from images.

The results show that visual abstraction is not the only source of failure.

Even with oracle states, models can exhibit substantial degradation in rollout prediction and planning under unseen goals.

However, the failures are not uniform.

Free-space variants often preserve or improve planning performance relative to the base task. In contrast, contact-rich variants exhibit much larger prediction-error increases and consistent planning degradation.

These results suggest that **interaction complexity is an important factor in dynamics generalization**.

**[Figure 5 — Oracle-state rollout error and planning results]**

### Finding 2. End-to-End World Models Face Additional Representation Challenges

The difficulty becomes greater when world models must learn directly from visual observations.

AnyGoal controls generalization at the level of physical factors. Yet recombining familiar factors can produce visual observations that were not encountered together during training.

An encoder that correctly represents training observations may not recover the same underlying factors from these novel combinations.

As a result, end-to-end world models can fail even in cases where oracle-state predictors remain stable.

Our results indicate that **goal-shift failures can arise from both dynamics prediction and representation learning**.

**[Figure 6 — End-to-end world-model performance]**

### Finding 3. Representation Geometry Matters, Even Without Visual Shift

TwoRoom provides a particularly informative example.

Here, the agent position is the only changing physical factor. The goal-shifted positions remain within the training support, so the corresponding observations remain visually familiar.

Nevertheless, end-to-end models degrade while oracle-state predictors remain stable.

Our representation analysis suggests that learned latent spaces can organize the same underlying physical states with substantially different geometries.

Such differences can affect both prediction and planning.

This means that **familiar observations do not guarantee representations suitable for generalization**.

**[Figure 7 — TwoRoom results and latent-space visualization]**

### Finding 4. Prediction Accuracy and Planning Success Are Not Interchangeable

Rollout prediction evaluates the accuracy of predicted trajectories, while MPC planning only needs to find an action sequence that reaches the goal.

Free-space tasks may admit many successful trajectories, allowing planning to tolerate imperfect predictions.

Contact-rich tasks often allow fewer viable action sequences because success depends on correctly modeling object interactions.

We therefore observe cases where rollout error and planning performance diverge, particularly in free-space variants.

These differences reinforce the importance of evaluating world models through **both prediction and downstream control**.

## 6. Toward World Models That Generalize Compositionally

What would make compositional generalization possible?

Our results motivate two distinct challenges.

**Identifying reusable dynamics.** A model must learn action-conditioned mechanisms from goal-directed trajectories without relying solely on behavior-policy regularities. This becomes especially difficult when action effects vary with physical state and interaction regime.

**Generalizing beyond training combinations.** Even correctly identified mechanisms need not generalize to unseen state–action compositions without additional structural assumptions.

We outline one favorable theoretical setting based on **sparse local mechanisms**.

Suppose each next-state factor depends only on a small subset of the current state and action variables within a fixed interaction regime.

Then a globally novel state–action composition need not require extrapolating every local mechanism.

\[
\mathcal C^{\mathrm{te}}
\not\subseteq
\mathcal C^{\mathrm{tr}},
\qquad
\mathcal U_k^{\mathrm{te}}
\subseteq
\mathcal U_k^{\mathrm{tr}}
\quad \forall k.
\]

In other words, **the global combination can be new even when every local mechanism receives familiar inputs**.

Under appropriate identification and local-coverage assumptions, such structure provides a sufficient condition for compositional generalization.

This is not a complete solution. Identifying reusable mechanisms, discovering interaction regimes, and handling genuinely new local interactions remain important open problems.
